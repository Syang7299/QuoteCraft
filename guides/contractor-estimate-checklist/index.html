What to Include in a Contractor Estimate: Complete Checklist

Most estimate disputes aren't about the price - they're about what the price was supposed to cover. A complete estimate closes that gap before it opens.

QuoteCraft Guides · Updated September 2026

A one-line total with no breakdown might be faster to write, but it's the version most likely to get disputed, renegotiated, or lost to a competitor's more detailed bid. Here's what a complete estimate covers.

Header and identification

  • Business details - your company name, license number (if applicable), address, phone, and email
  • Client and project details - client name and the project address, if different from their billing address
  • Date issued and validity period - a common default is 30 days, after which pricing may need to be re-quoted
  • A clear label - "Estimate" or "Quote," not left ambiguous (see the FAQ below for why this matters)

Scope of work

  • Line-item breakdown by task or area, not just a single lump sum - this is also what lets a client compare your bid to a competitor's fairly
  • Materials: supply-and-install vs. line item - state clearly whether a price includes installation or materials only
  • Allowances for any material the client hasn't selected yet (e.g. "$40/sq ft allowance for flooring, to be finalized")
  • Explicit exclusions - what is NOT included, such as permits, demolition, or debris removal, stated as clearly as what is

Cost breakdown

A detailed estimate separates cost into its real components rather than hiding them inside one number - these are the same categories QuoteCraft's calculator breaks out automatically:

  • Labor - your fully burdened labor cost, not just wages (see our labor cost guide)
  • Materials - and subcontractor costs, if any portion of the job is subbed out
  • Overhead - your share of running the business, allocated to this job
  • Contingency - a buffer for the unexpected, especially on jobs with unknowns (older buildings, unopened walls)
  • Markup and profit - your business's actual margin on top of cost (see our markup vs. margin guide)

Terms and logistics

  • Payment schedule - deposit amount, milestone payments, and final payment terms
  • Timeline - estimated start date and duration, with any conditions that could shift it
  • Change-order process - how added or changed work will be priced and approved once the job is underway
  • Insurance and licensing - proof of coverage, especially for larger residential or commercial jobs
  • Signature lines - for both parties, with the date

The vague spots that cause disputes

A handful of details get left out more often than the rest, and they're disproportionately the ones that turn into arguments mid-project:

  • Who handles demolition and debris removal, and what happens to anything salvageable
  • Who pulls and pays for permits, and whether permit fees are included in the price
  • What happens if site conditions differ from what was assumed once walls or floors are opened up
  • Whether pricing on materials with long lead times is protected against cost escalation between signing and purchase

None of these need to be complicated - they just need to be written down. A single sentence addressing each one is usually enough to prevent the disagreement entirely.

FAQ

What is the most commonly missing item on a contractor estimate?

An explicit list of exclusions - what is NOT included. Vague scope is the single biggest source of disputes; if demolition, debris removal, permits, or unforeseen conditions aren't explicitly addressed, both sides tend to assume the other is covering it.

Should a contractor estimate include a validity period?

Yes. Material and labor costs change, so estimates should state how long the pricing is valid (commonly 30 days) and note that costs may be re-quoted if the client doesn't accept within that window.

What's the difference between an estimate and a quote?

An estimate is an approximate price based on known information and is typically non-binding. A quote (or bid) is a fixed price the contractor commits to for a defined scope. Many contractors use the words interchangeably, but a document that will be signed as a binding price should say "quote," not just "estimate."
