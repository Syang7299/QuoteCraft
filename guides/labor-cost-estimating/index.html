How to Estimate Labor Costs for a Construction Job

The hourly wage you pay someone is not what that hour actually costs you. Skip the burden rate and every estimate you write is quietly underpriced before you even get to markup.

QuoteCraft Guides · Updated September 2026

Labor is usually the single biggest line item in a contractor estimate, and it's also the one most likely to be underestimated - not because the hours are wrong, but because the hourly rate used to price them is.

Wage vs. true labor cost

What you pay someone per hour and what that hour actually costs your business are two different numbers. The gap is called your labor burden - payroll taxes, workers' compensation insurance, benefits, and a share of equipment and overhead. Your estimate needs the second number, not the first.

Fully burdened labor rate

Rate = Hourly Wage × (1 + Burden %)

Worked example

Hourly wage: $25/hour

Burden rate: 40% (roughly $4 payroll tax + $3 workers' comp + $2 benefits + $1 overhead allocation, per hour)

Fully burdened rate: $25 × 1.40 = $35/hour

That's the number to multiply against labor hours - using the $25 wage instead would understate this worker's true cost by 29%.

Typical burden rates by trade

Burden rates vary by trade, region, and whether your workforce is union or non-union. As a starting reference point:

TradeTypical burden rate
Concrete / Drywall35-45%
Electrical40-50%
MEP (mechanical/electrical/plumbing)45-55%
Roofing50-70%

Union labor typically runs 10-20 percentage points higher than non-union for the same trade in the same market, due to negotiated benefits and pension contributions. Treat these as starting points, not a substitute for your own payroll numbers - your actual insurance rates, benefits package, and local taxes are what should ultimately set your burden %.

From labor hours to a labor cost

Once you know your fully burdened rate, the remaining question is how many hours the job actually needs:

Labor hours needed

Hours = (Quantity ÷ Production Rate) × Crew Size

For example, if one carpenter frames roughly 100 sq. ft. per hour and the job is 2,000 sq. ft., that's 20 hours for one carpenter - or 60 total labor-hours with a 3-person crew working together. Multiply those labor-hours by your fully burdened rate to get the dollar figure for the Labor field in your estimate.

Where possible, base your production rates on your own crew's job-cost history rather than a generic table - the gap between a published average and what your specific crew actually does on a specific site is usually the biggest swing factor in the whole estimate. Real-world conditions (weather, site access, coordination with other trades) also reduce output below the "ideal" rate, which is part of why most estimators build in a labor contingency rather than pricing to the theoretical minimum.

FAQ

What is a fully burdened labor rate?

It's an employee's hourly wage plus all the indirect costs of employing them - payroll taxes, workers' comp, benefits, and equipment/overhead allocation. Fully Burdened Rate = Hourly Wage × (1 + Burden %). A $25/hour wage with a 40% burden rate costs you about $35/hour in reality.

What is a typical labor burden rate in construction?

Industry sources commonly put overall construction labor burden in the 35% to 60% range, varying by trade - for example roughly 40-50% for electrical, 35-45% for concrete and drywall, and 45-55% for MEP. Union labor typically runs 10-20 percentage points higher than non-union for the same trade.

How do I turn labor hours into a labor cost for an estimate?

Estimate the labor hours needed (quantity divided by your crew's production rate, multiplied by crew size), then multiply those hours by your fully burdened hourly rate. That total becomes the Labor figure you enter into your estimate.
