Markup vs. Profit Margin for Contractors: Why They're Not the Same Number

A 30% markup does not give you a 30% profit margin. It gives you about 23%. That seven-point gap is one of the most common - and most expensive - pricing mistakes in the trades.

QuoteCraft Guides · Updated September 2026

If you build your quotes by adding a flat percentage on top of cost and call that your "profit margin," you're not alone - and you're probably underpricing every job you send out.

The core mistake

Markup is a percentage added on top of your cost to set your selling price. Profit margin is the percentage of your final selling price that ends up as profit. They sound interchangeable. They're not, because they're calculated against two different numbers - cost for markup, price for margin - and price is always the bigger number once markup is added.

Markup formula

Price = Cost × (1 + Markup%)

Profit margin formula

Price = Cost ÷ (1 − Margin%)

Worked example

Job cost: $8,000

Apply a 15% markup: Price = $8,000 × 1.15 = $9,200 → profit is $1,200, which is actually a 13.0% margin, not 15%.

Apply a true 15% margin instead: Price = $8,000 ÷ 0.85 = $9,411.76 → profit is $1,411.76, a genuine 15% of the final price.

Same 15%, two different formulas, a $212 gap on one mid-size job. Multiply that across a year of estimates and it adds up to real money left on the table.

The formula behind this table: Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup). To go the other direction - find the markup needed to hit a target margin - flip it: Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin). For example, to actually land a 20% margin, you need roughly a 25% markup, not 20%.

Which one should you actually use?

Use markup while you're building the estimate - it's the natural way to go from a known cost to a selling price. Use margin when you're evaluating how the job actually performed, or when you're setting a target for your business overall, since margin is what actually shows up as profit on your books relative to revenue.

Industry benchmarks commonly place a healthy contractor net profit margin in the 10-20% range: 10-15% is typical in competitive markets, and 20%+ often reflects a highly efficient operation or premium positioning. Where your business should land depends on your overhead, region, and trade - but knowing which number you're actually looking at is the precondition for hitting any target at all.

FAQ

Is a 30% markup the same as a 30% profit margin?

No. A 30% markup on cost works out to roughly a 23% profit margin. Markup is calculated on your cost; margin is calculated on your final selling price, and those are different numbers because the price already includes the markup.

What is a good profit margin for a contractor?

Industry benchmarks commonly place a healthy contractor net profit margin in the 10% to 20% range, with 10-15% typical in competitive markets and 20%+ often reflecting highly efficient operations or premium positioning. Your right number depends on your overhead, region, and trade.

How do I convert markup to margin?

Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup). For example, a 25% markup converts to a 20% margin (0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 0.20). To go the other way, Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin).
